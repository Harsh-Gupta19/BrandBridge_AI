The problem with just rotating people

Cross-training is the right ambition. It is also the thing most likely to sink a 21-day project, and it fails in two specific ways that are worth naming before designing around them.

Failure one — nobody owns the shape

If authorship rotates and nothing else does, every domain drifts. The schema grows three naming conventions. The feature builder gets a fourth signature. Nobody notices that a rule was duplicated, because the person who wrote it in week 3 was somewhere else in week 5. By week 6 the integration cost exceeds the remaining capacity.

Failure two — the viva gets worse, not better

The individual viva asks two questions: explain this system and what did you build and why. Broad rotation improves the first answer and can destroy the second. Five people who each touched everything shallowly and decided nothing is a worse outcome than five specialists, and examiners notice it immediately.

The design response

Separate the two things that lane ownership bundles together. Stewardship — accountability for a domain's integrity across all seven weekends — does not rotate. Authorship — who actually writes the code this weekend — does. Everyone keeps one domain they can defend in depth, and everyone still builds in all five.

Three mechanisms, in order of importance

  1. Vertical slices instead of horizontal tasks. The single biggest cross-training lever, and it costs nothing. Assign “creator rate card, end to end” — migration, model, repository, service, endpoint, screen, test — rather than “build the rate card API”. One person walks the whole stack for one feature. A slice is finishable inside one weekend, which is exactly what a three-day cadence needs.
  2. Pair-and-swap on Saturdays. Each weekend every person spends their deepest block in a domain they do not steward, with the steward pairing for the first two hours and reviewing on Sunday. Four rounds put everyone through all four other domains.
  3. Teaching artifacts. Every visit ends with ten lines in docs/domains/<domain>/field-notes.md: what I built, what surprised me, where the traps are. This is how knowledge outlives the rotation — and it is also, almost for free, the individual contribution log the viva needs.
Why the layer contract makes this safe

Rotation only works if a newcomer cannot break something they do not understand. The layer contract in Part 3 of the Build Manual is what provides that: routers cannot query, services cannot write SQL, ML modules cannot see a Session. A visitor writing their first endpoint in an unfamiliar domain is boxed in by the architecture rather than by their own caution. Enforce the contract strictly from weekend 1 and rotation becomes cheap; let it slide and rotation becomes dangerous.

The rotation

Four rounds, built so that each person visits each of the four domains they do not steward exactly once, and every domain receives exactly one visitor per round. No gaps, no collisions, no scheduling argument.

Read the matrix in one line

You steward your home domain every single weekend. On weekends 2–5 you additionally spend Saturday building in one other domain, moving one step around the ring each round. Weekend 1 is a shared build with no rotation; weekend 6 you choose your own weakest area; weekend 7 everyone is home and nothing new is written.

The weekend shape under rotation

The Friday–Sunday rhythm from the execution plan is unchanged. What changes is that Saturday belongs to a different domain than Friday and Sunday do — roughly seven of every sixteen hours, or 44% of the project, is spent outside your home domain.

How to run the thirty-minute handover

This is the mechanism the whole model rests on, and it only works because it is prepared in advance. In weekend 1 every steward writes a one-page docs/domains/<domain>/README.md answering five fixed questions. From weekend 2 the handover is a walkthrough of that page plus the specific slice.

MinutesSteward does
0–5Open the domain README. Name the three files that matter most and what each is for.
5–12Run the domain's tests live. A visitor who has seen the tests pass knows what “working” looks like.
12–20Walk one existing example of the slice pattern end to end — the thing the visitor is about to copy.
20–26Name the traps. “Never commit inside a repository.” “The encoder is a singleton.” “Re-embed after any profile write.” These are the field notes from previous visitors.
26–30Agree the exact slice and its definition of done. Write it in the issue before you stop talking.

The five questions every domain README answers

  1. What is this domain responsible for, in two sentences, and what is explicitly not its job?
  2. Which three files should a newcomer read first, in what order?
  3. How do I run just this domain's tests, and what does a healthy run look like?
  4. What is the standard shape of a change here — the pattern to copy?
  5. What are the three ways people have broken this so far?

Rules that keep rotation safe

#RuleWhy it exists
1Visitors implement; stewards decide. A visitor may write any code in the domain's established pattern. A visitor may not introduce a new pattern, rename a shared concept, change a schema, or add a dependency.This is the entire defence against architectural drift, and it costs nothing.
2The steward is a required reviewer on every PR in their domain, and reviews it the same weekend.A review on Wednesday is a review nobody acts on.
3Schema changes go through D1 regardless of who wrote them. A visitor may author a migration; Harshit merges it.One inconsistent migration costs more than a weekend of rotation gains.
4One slice per visit, agreed in writing before Saturday starts. If it is not finishable in nine hours, it is the wrong slice.Unfinished visiting work is the worst kind — the author leaves and the steward inherits half a thing.
5Tests are the handover. A visiting PR without tests does not merge, even if the code is right.The tests are what let the steward maintain it after the visitor moves on.
6Field notes are part of the PR, not an afterthought.Ten lines from the last visitor are worth more to the next one than an hour of the steward's time.
7If a weekend gate fails, the next weekend's rotation is cancelled. Everyone stays home until the gate passes.Rotation is the flexible thing in this plan. The gates are not.
8Nobody visits the domain that is behind. If D3 is a weekend late, the D3 steward keeps their Saturday.Teaching costs the steward time exactly when they have none.

Why this trade is worth making

Sixty hours buys five engineers who can each build across the whole stack, review any pull request in the repository, and answer a question about any part of the system in the viva. It costs six stretch features that no examiner will ask about. For a course called AI/ML in Practice, the first outcome is closer to the point of the exercise than the second.

When to stop rotating

Have this conversation on the Sunday of weekend 3 and again in weekend 5, deliberately, before the signals become obvious. Abandoning rotation is a legitimate decision; drifting out of it silently is not.

SignalWhat it meansDo this
Two gates failed in a rowThe plan is over capacity, and rotation is the cheapest thing to removeCancel rotation for weekends 5 and 6. Keep weekend 1's mob knowledge and the field notes. Say so in the report as a measured decision.
Visiting PRs consistently need reworkThe slices are too large, or the domain README is too thinHalve the slice size and rewrite that domain's README before cancelling anything. This is usually a briefing problem, not a people problem.
A steward spends Saturday teaching instead of buildingThe two-hour pairing cap is not being keptEnforce the cap. After two hours the visitor works from the README and the tests, and asks asynchronously.
Someone rates themselves 0 in a domain after visiting itThey watched rather than builtGive them the keyboard for the whole of their next visit and give the steward a different task in the same room.
Merge conflicts rising sharplyTwo people are editing the same files from different modesSequence the slices so a visitor and a steward are never in the same module on the same weekend. This is a Friday planning fix.
It is weekend 7—Rotation is already over by design. Everyone is home.

What this gives you in the viva

The individual examination is where this model pays off, and it pays off only if each person can do both halves.

Depth — from stewardship

“I own the modelling domain. I chose harmonic mean over the arithmetic and minimum alternatives, here is the comparison on held-out data, and here is the case where it costs us relevance.” That answer needs seven weeks of continuous ownership, which is exactly what stewardship preserves.

Breadth — from rotation

“I also built the OAuth callback and the rate-card screen, so I can tell you why the token never reaches React and why the rate card is replace-as-a-set rather than row-by-row.” That answer needs to have written the code, not to have read about it.

Judgement — from the field notes

Seven weekends of ten-line entries give each person a concrete record of what surprised them and what they got wrong. “What did not work?” is the question that separates answers, and it cannot be improvised.

The team-level claim

Three dated snapshots of the skills matrix turn “we all learned a lot” into a measured outcome with a method behind it. Put the weekend 1 and weekend 7 matrices side by side in the report appendix.
